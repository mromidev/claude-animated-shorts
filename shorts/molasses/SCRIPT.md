# "The Great Molasses Flood": original script v1 (2026-11-21)
Format: ~65 s vertical cartoon short, ~3.7 words/s, one steady narrator voice, our own characters and story. English only, no captions.

## Facts (with sources)
- 15 January 1919, about 12:30 pm, Purity Distilling Company tank on Commercial Street, North End, Boston (owned by US Industrial Alcohol). Built 1915, about 50 ft (15 m) tall, 90 ft wide, 2.3 million US gallons. (Wikipedia; boston.gov "100 years ago today molasses crashes through Boston's North End")
- The tank leaked from the start and "rumbled"; neighbours (including children with sticks and buckets) collected the molasses oozing from it. (Britannica "Great Molasses Flood")
- The company painted the tank brown after locals saw molasses seeping through the seams, instead of repairing it. (boston.gov)
- The air warmed from about 2 °F to about 40 °F in the days before the failure; warmer molasses had recently been added. (Wikipedia; Farmers' Almanac)
- Witnesses heard the rivets pop like machine-gun fire as the tank split. (Wikipedia; History.com)
- Wave about 25 ft (8 m) high at its peak, about 35 mph (56 km/h); it bent the girders of the Atlantic Avenue elevated railway. (Wikipedia; Britannica)
- As the evening air cooled, the molasses grew far more viscous and trapped people; rescuers searched for days. (Wikipedia; 2016 study by N. Sharp et al., Harvard, on cold viscosity)
- 21 people died, about 150 were injured. (Wikipedia; NPR)
- Class action (Dorr v. US Industrial Alcohol): in 1925 the company was found liable and paid about $628,000. The case led to stricter rules: engineers and architects had to check and sign off on structures like this. (sec.state.ma.us; CBS8 "strict construction codes were started by a giant tidal wave of molasses")
- Residents said the area smelled of molasses on hot summer days for decades. (Wikipedia)
- The idiom "slow as molasses in January" (19th-century American) — the flood happened in January.

## Script
In 1919, a wave of molasses taller than a house tore through Boston faster than a galloping horse.
Down by the harbor stood a brand new steel tank, five stories high, holding two point three million gallons of the stuff.
It leaked so badly that the neighbors filled buckets straight from the seams.
The company's fix? They painted the tank brown, so the leaks wouldn't show.
Then one January morning, the frozen city suddenly warmed up.
The tank groaned, and its rivets started popping like gunshots.
Just after lunch, the whole tank split wide open.
A brown wave twenty-five feet high swept down the street at thirty-five miles an hour, and bent the elevated railway like a paperclip.
Luckily, the cold evening air slowed the molasses down.
Unluckily, the cold evening air slowed the molasses down.
You see, molasses is a lot like honey. Warm from the microwave, it pours like juice. Straight from the fridge, it barely moves at all.
So as the sun went down, the whole flood set like glue around everyone still inside it.
Twenty-one people died. After years in court, the company had to pay, and America got stricter rules: big tanks need an engineer to check the math and sign their name on it.
For decades, the neighborhood smelled sweet on every hot summer day.
And it all happened in January, the one month molasses is famous for being slow.
